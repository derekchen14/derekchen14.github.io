## The Cold-Climate Doubt
The claim that heat pumps stop working below freezing comes from 1980s single-speed compressors that lost most of their capacity by 20°F and leaned on resistance backup around the clock. Those units ran at one speed, full blast or off, and once the outdoor coil couldn't pull enough heat the job fell to electric strip heat, which drove up the power bill. That doubt is forty years old, and contractors still quote a furnace as backup even where winters rarely dip into single digits.

The installation numbers contradict the doubt. Maine now installs more heat pumps per capita than many warmer states, and Norway has one of the highest adoption rates in the world despite its climate (International Energy Agency, *The Future of Heat Pumps*, 2022). Scandinavian manufacturers have refined cold-climate compressors since the 1990s, decades before the technology got attention in the US.

## How Modern Heat Pumps Perform
Cold-climate inverter units hold a coefficient of performance above 2 down to -13°F in third-party testing, a number that would have sounded absurd to anyone running a 1980s unit. The gain comes from variable-speed compressors paired with enhanced vapor injection, a second refrigerant path that lets the compressor pull extra low-pressure gas and keep output up as the outdoor air thins. Instead of one fixed speed, the compressor ramps from around 20 percent capacity on a mild day to well over 100 percent on the coldest night.

NEEP's Cold Climate Air Source Heat Pump list tracks capacity retention at 5°F and -5°F, and the top units still deliver 70 to 80 percent of rated capacity at 5°F. Field studies run by Oak Ridge National Laboratory found similar retention in real installations, not just lab conditions. A homeowner in Minneapolis running one at -10°F will see defrost cycles kick in more often and the fan work harder, but the unit keeps producing heat.

## Sizing and Backup
Sizing a heat pump for the single coldest day of the year sounds careful, but it wastes efficiency on the other 350 days when that extra capacity sits unused. A Manual J load calculation, the standard published by the Air Conditioning Contractors of America, should drive the sizing decision, not a contractor's rule of thumb. An oversized unit short-cycles, turning on and off in bursts that never let the compressor settle into its efficient range.

Backup resistance heat belongs below the unit's balance point, triggered by actual need, not switched on by default the moment it gets cold. That backup is usually a 5kW to 10kW electric strip in the air handler, wired to activate only when the compressor can't keep up. A correctly sized unit covers 95 percent of hours in most US climates without calling on backup at all.

## What the Skeptics Get Right
Performance and cost both get worse during the coldest snaps, and skeptics are right to point that out. Below about -15°F even a good cold-climate unit's coefficient of performance drifts down toward 1.5, and if a house needs a panel upgrade to support the backup strips, the upfront cost can run a few thousand dollars past a like-for-like furnace swap.

That cost concentrates in a small number of hours each year. Sealing air leaks and adding attic insulation does more for comfort on a cold night than any compressor upgrade, and most states now offer rebates covering part of the panel work. The skeptics have the extremes right and are wrong to treat the extremes as the whole story.

## Running Costs in Winter
At $0.15 per kWh and a coefficient of performance of 2.5, a heat pump heats for less per BTU than oil or propane, and the gap widens as the electric grid gets cleaner. Because dropping winter temperatures force the compressor to work harder, a January heating bill can look much higher than a November bill for the same house. Running costs rise as the temperature drops, since the compressor works harder for the same coefficient of performance, so a January bill and a November bill from the same house can look very different. Compare against local fuel prices, since propane costs vary widely by region. My own numbers back this up: a properly sized heat pump beat my old propane furnace by about 30 percent over one winter, counting the handful of nights the backup strips ran.
